## Our Current Global State & History


{{< figure src="/images/blog/windows95.jpg" alt="windows" width="600" caption="Launch day of Microsoft Windows 95" >}}

By 2026, we’ve reached a point where instant, personalized information isn't just a luxury—it’s the baseline. But as Artificial Intelligence moves faster than our ability to regulate it, the world is left scrambling to figure out the actual moral cost of this progress. To understand how we got here, we have to look back to a massive turning point: July 14, 1995.

When Microsoft launched Windows 95, it did more than just update an interface; it broke down the walls of what was technologically possible. By moving away from the old MS-DOS framework, it became a massive catalyst for the internet as we know it, introducing trailblazing features like DHCP and dial-up networking that brought the world online.
However, with this rapid development comes a heavy responsibility to adapt. When our tools outpace our safety measures, the results can be catastrophic. Look at the May 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack: a ransomware group called DarkSide managed to shut down the largest fuel pipeline in the U.S. simply because the system lacked basic multifactor authentication. That five-day shutdown led to a state of emergency, a $4.4 million ransom, and a massive hit to the company’s reputation.

This incident was a wake-up call that technology isn't just about innovation—it’s about security and ethics. As we dive deeper, we see a growing divide in how different cultures handle this power. While China leans into state-driven AI integration, the European Union is fighting for a more human-centric, rights-based approach.

If we take into consideration the presence of accelerating change in respect to technology, it is our responsibility as humans to adapt consequentially to our own creations, and mandatory when our technological advancements create divide or confusion in other parts of our lives. I think it is worthwhile to ponder how different areas of our world adjust to society's lightspeed developments, as in many cases it can place a country within an unfavorable situation. For example, in May 2021, a ransomware group called DarkSide gained access to Colonial Pipeline, the largest products pipeline in the entire United States which transports over 100 million gallons of fuel every day across the entire East Coast. The attack was so severe that it resulted in the Biden Administration declaring a state of emergency. DarkSide accessed the network with relative ease as the system did not have multifactor identification protocols in place. As a result, the pipeline shutdown spanned over a 5-day period between May 7-12 of 2021. The ransom of the access was set at 75 bitcoin, approximately worth $4.4 million. The Department of Justice recovered 3/4ths of the ransom, but the company experienced severe reputational decline as it is highly discouraged by the FBI to provide such payments to these types of organizations.

Shortly after the attack, various plaintiffs in the class action lawsuit sued Colonial Pipeline for negligence, in which the statement assessed that the incident was responsible for the negative impact towards 11,000 fuel retailers across the country. While the suit was unsuccessful, the cost expenditure was monumental for the company, and during the brutal aftermath the US Department of Transportation issued a compliance order which mandated Colonial Pipeline a civil penalty of $1 million USD.

## Societal Necessity

{{< figure src="/images/blog/qiang.jpg" alt="China" width="600" caption="Current Chinese Premier Li Qiang" >}}

The way technology has polarized the globe over the past 20 years is remarkable, with a portion of the world population believing that technology should be strictly controlled under ethical laws pertaining to personal data and should have no place in authoritative roles. The way that technology grows is not only global however, as one can make the case that modern technology must also fit the mould of the system it occupies. For instance, in places like China there exists a semi-reliant nature for Artificial Intelligence to operate within China's titanic infrastructure. (“China’s “AI+” Drive Aims for Integration across Sectors: A Wake-up Call for Europe”) This reliance manifests as Industrial and Government Integration, as China is pushing towards an infusion of AI into core sectors including manufacturing, finance, logistics and public administration. Arguably most interesting however, is the full-fledged use of AI in the healthcare sector. (Riehl) 

Specifically, the Zhongshan Hospital in Shanghai is developing an AI programmed to act as a pilot which aggregates medical and personal information to assess and recommend certain healthcare and lifestyle interventions to individuals, and even more entire hospitals across China. Additionally, the growth and importance of AI in China allows them to create distinct AI-driven products and services which other regions are yet to explore. In fact, on July 26th, 2025, Chinese Premier Li Qiang unveiled China's comprehensive 13-point plan of Action for AI Governance at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference. (“South China Morning Post”)

The Plan of Action contained aspects concerning the national relationship with AI, as Li Qiang described the rapid development and monetization of AI as an "exclusive game" in which only a few players will have a stake in the sector. Within the plans' governance framework, Open-source development is a major point of focus as the plan advocates for building reliable open-source platforms to lower the floor for technological innovation and application. In turn, enhancing the inclusiveness of AI services across the country. Outlined across a ten-year timespan, this cohesive plan hopes to implement sustainable AI initiatives such as energy-efficient chips, low power computing and precise benchmarks to reduce AI's carbon footprint. As a result of the plan, China aims to push for full integration in their economy by 2035. The way that China views the development and popularization of AI is seen more positively is potentially due to the structure of China's society. For instance, China is very keen on improving their infrastructure as a whole population rather than implementing codes towards individual privacy, as although they set comprehensive data protection laws in 2021 the Chinese government still has the authority to access personal information against personal privacy. 

## The Western Perspective

{{< figure src="/images/blog/unitedNations.jpg" alt="UN" width="600" caption="U.N Security Council Meeting, 2023" >}}

Contrasting the state-driven AI machine that China operates, the UN and European Union approach the situation in a more holistic manner. If we look at China's usage of Artificial Intelligence within its polarizing social credit systems, one can make the argument that AI used in this fashion is the antithesis of respecting human rights and privacy. To combat the potential exploitation of AI, the European Union, similarly to China, looks to ensure that open-source platforms are readily available to the public. Unlike China however, in 2024 the EU implemented the 2024 EU Act. The act operates as the world's first comprehensive AI Law, with heavy scaling towards ethics and risk-containment as it pertains to the expansion of Artificial Intelligence technology. The act's most important framework goes as follows:

  - Unacceptable risk: Prohibition of using manipulative behavior, prohibition of criminal profiling, etc.

  - High-Risk: Systems MUST allows human oversight

  - Limited Risk: Users must be informed they are interacting with AI

 If we analyze the laws' risk-containment framework we see that the unfavorable ethical outcomes of AI can be limited to a degree. For instance, the law outlines that systems that pose a visible and noticeable threat to human rights such as social scoring and using biometrics identification in public settings must be banned across the European Union. Despite the several positive influences from the Law, there are still points of contention within it. For example, the lower risk systems are susceptible to bias as the use and distribution of deepfakes, and synthetic information is classified as minimal-risk systems. In general, The EU is more concerned with its ability to act the leader in the ethical application of Artificial Intelligence, opting for privacy and maintenance of human rights rather than large-scale infrastructure development at a cost. (European Commission)





## Where it Leaves Us

{{< figure src="/images/blog/meeting.jpg" alt="meeting" width="600" caption="U.N Security Council Meeting, 2023" >}}

As AI builds its monumental capacity, it is for us as a human society to realize that not every country can benefit from the same solution. When a country that prioritizes economic and technological growth first, one cannot expect that a technology with unlimited financial potential would be severely limited in this system. On the other side of the coin, countries that strive for ethical and social progression will not thrive under the monetary ideals that China appreciates at it concerns AI.  
The important thing that most countries must recognize, fitting system or otherwise, is that AI has the potential to deviate to unfathomable outcomes, and potentially reach maleficence if not controlled at the very least. Take for example blue washing at events or summits, where companies/organizations display their ethical activism but continue selling personal user data to authority/third party organizations contradicting their position. Similarly green washing parties promote themselves as environmentally sound when they exploit these same issues behind the scenes for monetary gain. Another example of unethical exploitation of AI that occurs is ethics lobbying. In short, ethics lobbying is the practice of using ethics as a justification to bypass developmental technology laws.




{{< figure src="/images/blog/DeepSeek.jpg" alt="meeting" width="600" caption="DeepSeek founder, Liang Wenfeng, 2025" >}}

 Similarly to blue and green washing but more specifically, the company in question would deliver a initiative effectively proposes a conditional ethical code that fits their financial narrative. The company would do this by funding certain think tank events which align with their viewpoints. Companies who participate in these sorts of ethics lobbying ideas normally would vouch against binding regulation such as the law passed by the EU. Many companies’ pair ethics lobbying with a term known as ethical shirking, whereby companies justify apparent ethical exploitation with an indirect appeal to authority in where they claim that if a particular government were to enforce a law that inserts a restriction that the company in question would then only comply. In other words, many of these companies know the consequences of their behaviour but continue to do so in a pursuit of financial enrichment. These ethically dishonest practices are inclusive to all parties with a vantage point in the AI industry and is being done by many.
 
 
  For example, Google has been involved heavily with AI ethics lobbying as they've continuously worked with government officials with their Government Affairs and Public Policy team. (Harshawn Ratanpal) Google additionally works with think tanks and NetPAC to advance its policy goals. In fact, Google opposed certain data sharing based bills like Assembly Bill 566, which requires browsers to opt out of third-party data sharing. (“Google’s Digital Policy Power: Public Policy Shaping and Private Policy Making – the Tech Lobby”) These unethical practices normalize the exploitation of services which justifies the means to an end. We as a human race need to realize that our own exciting innovation, regardless of malicious intent, needs a limiter to keep our own creations from hurting us ethically, as justification can be used cross-referencing innovative arguments.
